Pipeline Capacity and Royalty Revenue

Following Canadian crude from pipeline capacity to U.S. refineries to Alberta's royalty revenue.

Context

Why it mattered

Alberta's oil is landlocked. What it earns depends on how much the pipelines can carry, where they go, and what buyers will pay. This report follows the barrels from the pipe to the provincial treasury.

It combines Canada Energy Regulator throughput data for the Enbridge Mainline, Trans Mountain and the TCPL Mainline, U.S. Energy Information Administration import data by refining region, and Government of Alberta royalty records, all pulled and charted in R.

The question
How do pipeline capacity, dependence on U.S. buyers and royalty revenue fit together in Canada's oil economy?
  • Are the main export pipelines running full?
  • Did the Trans Mountain Expansion open new markets?
  • Where does Alberta's royalty revenue come from now?
Approach

How I solved it

01 · Sources

Three agencies, one workflow.

Pipeline throughput and capacity, U.S. crude imports by refining district, and a royalty series back to 1970.

02 · Capacity

The main corridor runs close to full.

Enbridge Mainline shipments at Gretna have tracked just under available capacity for years, so new supply needs new pipe.

03 · New route

Trans Mountain finally reaches tidewater.

Sumas has carried about 200 thousand barrels a day into Washington State and Burnaby about 100. Westridge tanker exports were small until the expansion opened, then surged in 2024 and 2025.

04 · Buyers

U.S. Midwest refineries run on Canadian heavy.

In the PADD 2 Midwest region, Canadian heavy crude supplies almost all imports, and it has been displacing other countries in other regions too.

05 · Royalties

From gas to oil sands.

Natural gas royalties led through the 1990s and 2000s. Oil sands royalties overtook them in the 2010s and drove the 2022 surge.
